# Stage 8: Menu Development

**Timeline:** Months 6–9 (runs parallel to build-out)
**Goal:** Build a menu that guests love, that your kitchen can execute consistently under pressure, and that hits your food cost targets every service.

---

## Menu Is Your Product

The menu is not a list of dishes — it's the core product-market fit test of your entire business. Every decision (concept, kitchen layout, equipment, staffing, supplier relationships) flows from the menu. Build it deliberately, test it relentlessly, and trim it ruthlessly.

---

## Menu Design Principles

### 1. Menu Size: Smaller Is Almost Always Better
Every item on your menu:
- Requires training (every employee must know it)
- Adds inventory complexity (more ingredients = more waste)
- Adds prep labor
- Increases ticket times if it's slow to execute

**Rule of thumb:** A fast casual should launch with 8–14 items. A full-service restaurant: 20–30 items including starters, mains, and desserts. You can always add later. You can't easily remove popular items without backlash.

### 2. Menu Engineering Matrix (Run This Before You Finalize Anything)
For each item, calculate:
- **Food cost %:** Ingredient cost ÷ menu price × 100
- **Contribution margin:** Menu price − ingredient cost
- **Popularity:** How often does this sell vs. other items?

| Category | High Margin | Low Margin |
|---|---|---|
| **High Popularity** | ⭐ Stars — Feature heavily | 🐴 Plowhorses — Reprice or reformulate |
| **Low Popularity** | 🧩 Puzzles — Reposition or promote | 🐕 Dogs — Remove |

Launch with a menu where at least 70% of items are projected Stars or Plowhorses.

### 3. Anchor Pricing
Use menu price anchoring to make your target items seem reasonable:
- Include 1–2 premium items priced higher than your target check average — they make your core items feel like good value
- Remove dollar signs from menus (reduces price-focused thinking, increases spend ~8%)
- Use price endings strategically ($18.95 feels less than $19; $19 feels more upscale than $18.95 — pick a lane)

### 4. The 30% Rule for San Jose
Your food cost per item should not exceed 30–32% of the menu price. In San Jose:
- You can and should price 15–25% above national averages — the market supports it
- A dish that costs $5 to make should be on the menu at $16.50+
- A dish that costs $8 to make should be at $26.50+

If an ingredient costs too much to hit this target, find a substitute or don't put the dish on.

## Recipe Development Process

### Step 1: Concept-Driven Brainstorm
Start with your cuisine concept and customer persona (from Stage 1). List every dish that:
- Fits the concept authentically
- Your team can execute consistently
- Uses ingredients you can source reliably in San Jose
- Hits a 28–32% food cost target at market pricing

### Step 2: Standardized Recipe Development
For every item, create a standardized recipe card that includes:
- **Ingredients with exact weights** (not "a handful of" — use grams or ounces)
- **Step-by-step prep method**
- **Portion weight / yield**
- **Plating photo** (how the dish looks when it leaves correctly)
- **Allergen flags** (Big 9: milk, eggs, fish, shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soy, sesame)
- **Theoretical food cost** (per portion, calculated against your supplier pricing)

Use Claude to format your recipe notes into standardized recipe cards. Feed in your notes and ingredient list; Claude generates a formatted card. Load into Trainual for kitchen training.

### Step 3: Test Kitchen Sessions
Before finalizing the menu:
1. Cook every dish in your actual kitchen equipment
2. Time every dish from start to plate (ticket time target: under 8 minutes for fast casual; under 15 minutes for full-service)
3. Have 5–10 people outside your immediate circle taste and score everything (blind tasting)
4. Measure actual yield vs. theoretical — waste and trim shrinkage are usually 10–20% higher than you estimated
5. Adjust recipes based on feedback and actual food cost data

### Step 4: Pre-Opening Trial Runs
Before soft open, run 3–5 full service simulations with real dishes and a full team:
- Can every line cook execute every dish at the same quality level?
- What's the actual ticket time under pressure (10 orders simultaneously)?
- Where are the bottlenecks? (Usually: cold expo, fryer capacity, prep volumes)
- What does the plate look like after sitting in a delivery bag for 20 minutes? (Critical for DoorDash menu)

## Menu Engineering for San Jose Specifically

### High-Margin Items for Silicon Valley
These consistently work in the tech-adjacent San Jose market:

| Category | Item Type | Target Food Cost | Target Price | Margin |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Beverages | Specialty coffee, matcha, housemade drinks | 15–20% | $6–$9 | $4.80–$7.65 |
| Starters | Shareable small plates | 22–28% | $12–$18 | $8.64–$13.68 |
| Bowl dishes | Grain bowl, poke, rice bowl | 24–28% | $16–$22 | $11.52–$15.84 |
| Sandwiches / tacos | $20–$28% | $14–$20 | $10.08–$14.40 |
| Proteins (upsell) | Added protein to any dish | 25–30% | $4–$7 | $2.80–$5.25 |
| Desserts | Housemade pastries, ice cream | 18–25% | $8–$14 | $6.00–$11.34 |
| Alcohol | Beer, wine, cocktails | 18–28% | $8–$16 | $5.76–$13.12 |

**Beverages and alcohol are the highest-margin categories in any restaurant. They must be on your menu if your concept and license allow it.**

### Delivery Menu Optimization
For DoorDash/Uber Eats, your menu needs special consideration:
- Items must survive a 10–20 minute packaging and transit time
- No items that are crispy (sogginess kills reviews)
- Every item needs a good photo (AI-generated or professional)
- Delivery-exclusive items can command 10–15% premium
- Offer a "delivery bundle" — 2 entrees + a drink/dessert — to increase average order value
- Make sure packaging presents well when unboxed

## Allergen Management

California restaurants must disclose the presence of the Big 9 allergens. The legal exposure for allergen mistakes is significant.

### Systems to Build
- Every menu (print and digital) must list allergens or have a clear system for customers to ask
- Each standardized recipe card flags allergens
- Train every staff member to escalate all allergen inquiries to the manager or head cook — never guess
- Build color-coded allergen handling: dedicated cutting boards, separate prep utensils, separate expo lane for severe allergy orders

### Common San Jose Context
- High prevalence of vegetarian/vegan customers (tech demographic)
- Significant Hindu and Muslim community (beef and pork restrictions)
- Consider: always offering one vegan and one vegetarian option per category

## Seasonal Menu Planning

San Jose's climate allows for year-round outdoor sourcing, but seasonal menus:
- Reduce food cost during peak season for produce
- Create urgency and social media content ("limited time strawberry special")
- Signal freshness and quality to a food-literate customer base

**AI-Assisted Seasonal Planning:**
Each quarter, use Claude to generate a seasonal menu refresh based on:
- What produce is in peak season in Northern California this month
- Your existing menu item profitability data
- Customer feedback and review analysis

A quarterly refresh of 2–3 items keeps the menu feeling alive without the complexity of a full menu overhaul.

## Pricing Strategy

### San Jose Benchmarks (2025–2026)
| Concept Type | Average Entree Price |
|---|---|
| Ghost kitchen / delivery-first | $15–$22 |
| Fast casual | $14–$22 |
| Casual dining | $18–$32 |
| Upscale casual | $28–$50 |
| Fine dining | $50–$100+ |

### How to Set Prices
1. Calculate your actual food cost per portion (after waste/trim)
2. Divide by your food cost % target (0.30 for 30% target)
3. That's your **floor price** — never go below it
4. Research competitors' prices for similar items
5. Price slightly above the midpoint of comparable competitors
6. Test: run the item for 30 days; if it's a Dog (low popularity), either price it down, reposition it, or remove it

### Menu Price Increases
You will need to raise prices. California's minimum wage and food costs both rise annually. Build a process:
- Review pricing every 6 months
- Raise prices on lower-margin items first
- Batch increases: a 5% across-the-board increase every year feels less jarring than a 15% jump every 3 years
- Use AI to identify which items customers are least price-sensitive to (typically beverages, desserts, premium proteins)

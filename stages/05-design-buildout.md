# Stage 5: Design & Build-Out

**Timeline:** Months 5–9
**Goal:** Build a space that serves the food efficiently, creates the right guest experience, and passes every inspection without busting the budget.

---

## The Design Hierarchy

1. **Kitchen function** — Design the kitchen first; everything else fits around it
2. **Code compliance** — ADA, fire code, health code requirements are non-negotiable
3. **Guest flow** — How customers enter, order, wait, sit, and exit
4. **Brand atmosphere** — Aesthetic, lighting, materials
5. **Cost** — Value-engineer once you know what you need, not before

Most restaurant amateurs design in reverse order and pay for it.

---

## The Team You Need

### Architect (Required for Permits)
- Must be a licensed California architect for building permit drawings
- Look for one with restaurant experience — general commercial architects cost you more in revisions
- Cost: $5,000–$20,000 depending on scope
- In San Jose: Recommend getting 3 bids from firms that have done 3+ restaurant projects in the Bay Area

### Kitchen Equipment Dealer / Kitchen Designer
- Often free or low-cost through your equipment purchase relationship
- They understand hood sizing, gas loads, code requirements, and layout efficiency
- Companies like Ace Mart, Clark's Restaurant Equipment, or Burkett in the Bay Area often provide design services
- Their kitchen layout must be included in your health permit plan check

### General Contractor (GC)
- Must be licensed in California (check CSLB license at cslb.ca.gov)
- Get minimum 3 bids with identical scope of work
- Look for GCs with prior restaurant build-out experience — restaurants are more complex than office TI
- Include a 10–15% contingency in every GC contract
- Ask for references from restaurant clients and call them

### Interior Designer (Optional but Valuable)
- Handles FOH aesthetic: furniture, lighting, finishes, colors
- Many kitchen/equipment dealers can recommend restaurant-experienced designers
- Cost: $3,000–$15,000 or hourly ($75–$150/hr)
- Alternative: Use Canva + AI-generated mood boards to create a design brief, then a designer executes it

## Kitchen Design Principles

### The Kitchen Work Triangle (Simplified)
Minimize steps between three zones:
1. **Receiving / Cold Storage** — Where food arrives and is stored
2. **Prep** — Where food is prepared
3. **Cooking Line** — Where food is cooked and plated
4. **Expediting / Pass** — Where food goes to front of house

The shorter the path between these zones, the faster your kitchen operates and the fewer labor hours you need.

### Station Design
Design around your menu — don't design generically and then try to fit your menu in.

For a fast casual or quick-service concept:
- **Assembly line flow** (Chipotle model) is most efficient for high volume
- Minimize crossover of traffic between cook and server paths
- Build in clear marking for allergen separation if needed

For a full-service:
- Hot line: range, fryer, grill, salamander
- Cold line: garde manger, salad station
- Sauté station
- Expo/pass position is critical — this is where service quality lives

### Key Kitchen Specifications
- **Hood:** Type 1 (for grease-producing equipment); size to BTU load of your equipment
- **Fire suppression:** Required under Type 1 hood (Ansul or equivalent); $3,000–$8,000 to install
- **Grease trap:** Sized by Santa Clara County Environmental Health to your kitchen output; interior vs. exterior interceptors
- **Hand sink:** Within required distance of all food prep areas; cannot double as prep sink
- **3-compartment sink:** Wash, rinse, sanitize; must be properly sized and positioned
- **Walk-in cooler:** Standard size for a fast casual: 6×8 or 8×10; cost $8,000–$20,000 installed
- **Flooring:** Quarry tile or epoxy (smooth, cleanable, non-slip); do not use materials that trap grease

### Ghost Kitchen Considerations
If using a commissary kitchen, you design almost nothing — you rent a fully equipped space by the hour. Budget instead for:
- Branded packaging design
- Dedicated shelving/storage at the commissary (often rentable)
- Cold storage rental

## Front of House Design

### Guest Flow Priorities
- **Entry and wait area:** First impression; must feel welcoming within 3 seconds
- **Ordering flow:** Clear, no ambiguity about where to queue or how to order
- **Seating:** Maximize covers without making it feel cramped (15–18 sqft per cover is standard; tight is 12 sqft)
- **Sightlines:** Guests should be able to see the kitchen activity if it's clean — open kitchens increase perceived quality
- **Exit:** Clean and easy; don't make people hunt for the door

### Seating Capacity Math
| Space | Seats at 15 sqft/cover | Seats at 18 sqft/cover |
|---|---|---|
| 800 sqft FOH | 53 | 44 |
| 1,000 sqft FOH | 66 | 55 |
| 1,200 sqft FOH | 80 | 66 |
| 1,500 sqft FOH | 100 | 83 |

More seats is not always better — turn rate matters more than seat count.

### Acoustic Design
Often ignored; never forgiven. Loud restaurants drive away repeat customers, especially the 35+ demographic that spends more.
- Sound panels ($500–$3,000) dramatically reduce reverberation
- Ceiling tiles, fabric panels, banquettes, and carpet absorb sound
- Test noise levels during build-out; add panels before opening if needed

### Lighting
- Natural light wherever possible (drives positive reviews)
- Table-level lighting for dinner service: warm (2700–3000K), dimmer-controlled
- Work zone lighting in kitchen: bright, cool (5000K)
- LED throughout — California Title 24 energy code requires it

## Technology Infrastructure (Build-Out Phase)

Install these during construction — retrofitting is 3× the cost:

| System | Notes |
|---|---|
| POS terminal drops | Ethernet runs to every POS location (don't rely on WiFi alone) |
| Kitchen Display System (KDS) | Ethernet drop at cook line; replaces paper tickets |
| Security cameras | Exterior, BOH, register; 30-day storage minimum |
| WiFi infrastructure | Commercial-grade AP (Ubiquiti UniFi); separate guest and business networks |
| Sound system | In-wall or in-ceiling speakers; zone control |
| Digital menu board drops | If using digital boards, run HDMI + power before drywall |
| EV charging (if parking) | SJ has EV charging incentives; future-proofs for tech customers |

### Cost-Saving Strategies
1. **Take a former restaurant space** — saves $80,000–$200,000 in kitchen infrastructure
2. **Buy used equipment** — 40–60% savings; source from Restaurant Equipment World, CL Foodservice, Kairak (San Jose area)
3. **DIY FOH finish work** — Paint, furniture assembly, some millwork can be owner-installed to save GC markup
4. **Phase the build-out** — Open with minimal FOH; add aesthetic elements as revenue builds
5. **Negotiate TI allowance aggressively** — Every dollar from the landlord reduces your out-of-pocket

## Inspection Sequence

Understanding this sequence prevents delays:

1. **Building permit issued** → Construction starts
2. **Framing inspection** → Before drywall goes up
3. **Rough electrical inspection** → Before walls close
4. **Rough plumbing inspection** → Before walls close
5. **Insulation inspection** (if applicable)
6. **Hood installation inspection** → After hood is in, before fire suppression is charged
7. **Fire suppression inspection** → By fire marshal
8. **Final building inspection** → All work complete
9. **Certificate of Occupancy issued** → Space is legally occupiable
10. **Environmental Health final inspection** → Kitchen equipment installed, all food safety requirements met
11. **Health permit issued** → You can open

Missing any step means re-inspection, which adds 2–4 weeks. Sequence matters.

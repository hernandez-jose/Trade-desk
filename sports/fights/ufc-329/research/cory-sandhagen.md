## 1. Executive Summary

Cory Sandhagen (18-6) is the #4 ranked UFC bantamweight, a 34-year-old switch-stance striker from Aurora, Colorado who has risen through the division on the back of elite creative striking, but has now lost three consecutive fights to elite-level grapplers (Nurmagomedov, Dvalishvili) following four losses in which wrestling or grappling was the decisive factor. The Bautista rematch at UFC 329 on July 11, 2026 is framed publicly as a reset fight — Sandhagen was the original winner (R1 armbar, 2019) — but the structural picture is far more dangerous. Bautista (17-3) is a submission-oriented grappler who built an 8-fight win streak including victories over Jose Aldo, Ricky Simon, and Patchy Mix before his loss to Nurmagomedov at UFC 321 (October 2025). He has called this rematch his obsession for years. Sandhagen's 63-64% takedown defense and documented inability to manage elite wrestling pressure over extended periods is the central analytical concern. This is not a confidence-rebuilding tune-up — it is a structurally dangerous rematch where the favorite's primary liability is the opponent's primary weapon.

*(Sources: UFC Stats, Sherdog, Tapology, ESPN MMA, Yahoo Sports)*

## 4. Striking Analysis

### Offense (switch stance, flying knee, head kicks)

Sandhagen is legitimately elite in this category — arguably a top-2 offensive striking talent in the bantamweight division. Key weapons and mechanics:

**Switch stance system:** His primary differentiator. He starts orthodox, switches to southpaw mid-combination, and uses the transition itself as a timing attack — the hip rotation of the stance switch loads power on the lead hand while simultaneously changing the angle the opponent must track. Most opponents at bantamweight have limited reps against switch-stance fighters and struggle to maintain proper defensive alignment. He times stance switches off opponent reactions (parries, head movement, forward pressure) with precision.

**Flying knee:** Among the most dangerous flying knees in UFC history. The Edgar KO (0:28, February 2021) is the second-fastest flying knee finish in UFC history. He typically sets it up with a jab feint or step-in movement, disguising the knee behind the shoulder line. Mechanically, he launches from his rear leg in southpaw stance and drives the lead knee upward — the alignment is deceptive because it looks like a charging punch entry. This weapon is live against Bautista, who will be attempting to shoot or clinch repeatedly.

**Spinning techniques:** Spinning heel kick (finished Moraes, R2, 2020), spinning back-fist (employed regularly as a distance management tool and occasional finisher). These are genuine threats, not stylistic decoration — he has fight-finishing power in spinning attacks.

**Output:** 4.86 SLpM — top-5 in bantamweight division. He maintains this across full five-round fights, not just early rounds. Against Vera (106 significant strikes to Vera's 44 in 5 rounds) and Font (50-45 unanimous), his sustained volume is the dominant metric.

**Front kicks/teep:** Used effectively to maintain distance and disrupt opponent timing on shot attempts. Particularly relevant here because Bautista must close distance to execute takedowns, and Sandhagen's front kick game is a genuine deterrent at range.

*(Sources: Fight.TV Technical Analysis, High Altitude Martial Arts breakdown, Sherdog Scouting Report, UFC Stats)*

### Defense

Striking defense: 56-58% — above average for the division, not elite. He relies primarily on distance management (footwork, reach) rather than head movement or blocking. When opponents close distance, his defense degrades. Key documented vulnerabilities:

- **Petr Yan**: A spinning back-fist in Round 3 of UFC 267 shifted the fight decisively; Yan dropped him and the scorecards went 49-46 x3 after being more competitive early
- **TJ Dillashaw**: Hurt Sandhagen multiple times in their split decision; Sandhagen's chin held but he was rocked visibly
- **Dvalishvili**: Stung Sandhagen repeatedly when the fight hit the cage; the takedown threat also forced Sandhagen into defensive postures that compromised his offensive output

Against Bautista, the striking defense question is less central because Bautista is not a power striker — his striking is primarily used as an entry into grappling. Sandhagen's defensive striking will not be the decisive factor in this fight.

*(Sources: ESPN, CBS Sports, UFC Stats)*

## 5. Grappling Analysis

### Offensive Wrestling

Underrated and underused. Sandhagen has a legitimate college wrestling background (University of Northern Colorado) that rarely surfaces in his fights, but it re-emerged prominently against Rob Font (Nashville, Aug 2023) where he secured a takedown in each of five rounds en route to a 50-45 unanimous decision. This demonstrates the capability exists but he chooses not to deploy it consistently. Against Bautista, the strategic question is whether Wittman's camp has identified any offensive wrestling usage as a means of disrupting Bautista's timing or testing his base.

Offensive wrestling stats: 1.19-1.36 takedowns per 15 min, 33-34% accuracy — below average at elite level. His takedown offense is an occasional weapon, not a cornerstone.

*(Sources: UFC Stats, ESPN MMA Profile)*

### Defensive Wrestling — The Critical Vulnerability

This section carries the most weight for betting analysis.

**The pattern is unambiguous across four documented fights:**

1. **Aljamain Sterling (UFC 250, Jun 2020):** Sterling caught a kick, entered the clinch, took the back, and locked in an RNC within 1:28 of Round 1. Sandhagen was unconscious post-tap. This was his first elite grappling exposure in the UFC and established the vulnerability immediately.

2. **Umar Nurmagomedov (UFC Abu Dhabi, Aug 2024):** Nurmagomedov went 5-for-13 in takedown attempts but his grappling threat forced Sandhagen into defensive postures throughout. Nurmagomedov connected on 98 of 187 significant strikes (52%) while Sandhagen hit 71 of 159 (44%). The 50-45 scorecard reflects sustained control. Notably, Nurmagomedov nearly hit a Twister submission in Round 1 — Sandhagen defended it, but the proximity to a catastrophic submission loss against an elite grappler is worth noting.

3. **Merab Dvalishvili (UFC 320, Oct 2025):** The definitive data point. 20 takedowns in 5 rounds — a UFC bantamweight championship fight record. Sandhagen's coach Trevor Wittman had teased an "unconventional" gameplan of seeking grappling exchanges to disrupt Dvalishvili's rhythm, which failed comprehensively. Scorecard: 49-45, 49-45, 49-46. Sandhagen showed improved scramble ability and got back to his feet repeatedly, but the constant threat neutralized his offensive output. He was competitive in some standing exchanges but Dvalishvili's cardio and pace overwhelmed his recovery.

4. **Aljamain Sterling loss (see above)** — combined with the structural losses above, the takedown defense percentage (63-64%) understates the actual vulnerability because those numbers blend fights against strikers and grapplers. Against elite-level grappling specialists specifically, his ability to maintain position and generate offense collapses.

**Cage wrestling specifically:** Once opponents establish a clinch against the cage, Sandhagen's striking output drops to near zero. He cannot generate his unorthodox angles when plastered against the fence with a grappler on his hips. Dvalishvili exploited this for 25 minutes. Bautista's technical repertoire (RNC, back takes, submission setups) is most dangerous precisely from cage control and back-take positions.

**Takedown defense number in context:** 63-64% appears moderate, but the denominator matters — against pure strikers who occasionally shoot, this looks adequate. Against disciplined wrestlers who chain takedown attempts with cage control (the Dvalishvili model, the Nurmagomedov model, and to a lesser extent the Bautista model), the number degrades significantly in real-time.

*(Sources: CBS Sports UFC 320 results, Yahoo Sports UFC Abu Dhabi, Blood Elbow post-fights, UFC Stats)*

### Scrambles & Submissions

When Sandhagen does get taken down, he is an active defensive grappler — he scrambles to his feet quickly and is not passive off his back. He showed this against Dvalishvili (allowing 20 takedowns but limiting control time to 10:02 across 25 minutes by scrambling to his feet). He also defended Nurmagomedov's Twister attempt in R1. His scramble instincts are solid.

Submission offense: 3 career submission wins, 0.3 sub avg/15 min at UFC level. He submitted Bautista in their 2019 meeting via armbar from a scramble — a reminder that his offensive grappling can produce submissions when opportunities arise. However, his submission offense in recent fights has been effectively zero.

*(Sources: CBS Sports, UFC Stats)*

### Cage Wrestling

This is Sandhagen's single most dangerous zone of the Octagon. Once Bautista initiates cage wrestling, Sandhagen's effective fighting range collapses. Bautista's strategy in this fight will almost certainly center on cage control: use striking to close the distance, establish the clinch, pin Sandhagen to the fence, generate takedown or back take opportunities. Sandhagen's ability to create separation from the cage (underhooks, framing, lateral movement) will be the technical adjustment to watch in pre-fight camp intel.

## 6. Cardio & Durability

### Pace Sustainability

Elite. Sandhagen is one of the top cardio fighters in the bantamweight division. He maintains 4.86 SLpM across five full rounds — his output does not drop in rounds 4-5 against strikers. In five-round wins over Vera (5R), Font (5R), and his 2020 Moraes fight (where he was hurt early before finishing), his late-round production remained high. He has never been stopped due to cardio failure.

The important caveat: his cardio against wrestlers is measured differently. Against Dvalishvili, his sustained energy to scramble and recover from takedowns is what allowed him to keep the damage relatively contained (10:02 control time out of 25 minutes), even though he could not win rounds. The sustained defensive grappling effort is physically exhausting, but Sandhagen handles it better than most.

*(Source: UFC Stats, CBS Sports UFC 320 analysis)*

### Recovery After Damage

Moderate concern. He has been dropped (Yan, R3), hurt multiple times (Dillashaw), and absorbed significant punishment before finishing opponents (Moraes fight — he was rocked in R1 before finishing with a spinning heel kick in R2). His resilience when hurt is real — he does not panic and often finds counter-attacks from defensive positions. However, his chin is not elite-level durable. The Yan fight demonstrated he can be hurt by clean power shots from quality strikers. Against Bautista, the striking damage concern is lower (Bautista is not a one-punch KO artist), but he should absorb some punishment in the standup while defending grappling entries.

### Weight Cut Effects

Walk-around weight approximately 148-152 lbs, fighting at 135 lbs. This is a 13-17 lb cut — standard for elite bantamweights at his height and frame. He has scaled 149.2 lbs on fight night (post-rehydration), suggesting a moderate rehydration following a meaningful cut. No documented issues with missing weight or appearing depleted at weigh-ins in recent fights. His cardio performance in 5-round fights suggests the cut is not compromising his late-round energy.

At 34 years old, the cutting process may become harder to manage over time, but there is no evidence of weight cut problems entering this fight.

*(Sources: EssentiallySports physical profile, Sherdog, Bloody Elbow)*

### Injury History

No major documented injuries have derailed Sandhagen's career. He is an active competitor who has fought at a steady pace (typically 2-3 times per year at UFC level) without extended injury absences. He is not entering this fight with any publicly disclosed injury concerns (as of May 2026). His fight frequency suggests he manages training load effectively.

*(Sources: SI.com injury tag, Tapology fight history, RotoWire)*

## 7. Psychological Factors

### Fight IQ

High, with a documented weakness. Sandhagen has a psychology degree from CU Boulder and works with sports psychologists and meditation practitioners. He processes losses quickly and maintains composure in chaotic fight situations — best demonstrated by the Moraes fight, where he absorbed punishment in Round 1 and calmly waited for a counter-attack opportunity. His ability to execute unconventional techniques (flying knee timing, spinning heel kick setups) under pressure reflects genuine fight intelligence.

The documented weakness: complacency in rounds he perceives as winning. The Dillashaw split decision loss is the canonical example — multiple analysts scored it Sandhagen based on output, but passive moments in perceived winning rounds allowed Dillashaw to steal rounds on the cards. In a 3-round fight with a grappler, coasting is not possible — every round matters equally and a point of slippage can gift Bautista a round on the judges' cards.

*(Sources: National Sports Journalism Center, Umbo/Mindset Analysis, JRE MMA Show #167 summary)*

### Pressure Handling

Calm under pressure in striking exchanges. Sandhagen does not freeze or panic when hurt — the Moraes fight is the evidence. He thinks clearly when threatened and executes technical counters. However, under sustained wrestling pressure — the specific type that compromises his game — he has not demonstrated a convincing solution. In three consecutive fights against elite grapplers (Nurmagomedov, Dvalishvili), he was unable to impose his striking and lost clearly.

### Momentum (coming off Dvalishvili loss)

Sandhagen's post-UFC 320 statements show emotional maturity. He called Dvalishvili "a hell of a fighter and a great champion" and wrote on Instagram: "Nothing changes for me. It's all hard work till the day I hang it up. We'll still get to gold — even though the water is murky right now. I'll always have an unbreakable spirit until the day I die." He appeared publicly to process the loss in a healthy way and expressed immediate interest in fighting Sean O'Malley before the Bautista rematch was booked.

He has experienced back-to-back losses before (Yan and Dillashaw, both 2021) and bounced back with three consecutive wins. The pattern suggests resilience, but this loss was to the champion after a 5-round title fight — a more psychologically significant defeat than the Dillashaw split.

**Key question:** Has Sandhagen genuinely internalized the grappling defensive adjustments needed, or is the psychological response masking a strategic problem that has no available solution within his current skill set?

*(Sources: Yahoo Sports, MMA Mania, Middle Easy, BVM Sports)*

### Rematch Psychology

The 2019 fight gives Sandhagen a psychological edge in terms of precedent — he has beaten Bautista before and did so quickly. But this cuts both ways: fighters who lost embarrassingly in their first meeting and have obsessed over the rematch for years are historically among the most dangerous. Bautista has explicitly said he has "wanted this fight for years" and has built his post-UFC 321 recovery around calling out Sandhagen. A highly motivated opponent with a specific game plan versus a fighter who may not take the stylistic threat as seriously as warranted is a classic fade setup.

*(Sources: Yahoo Sports rematch announcement, Cage Side Press UFC Vegas 113 post-fight)*

## 8. Camp & Preparation

### Coaching Staff (Trevor Wittman — contextualized impact)

Trevor Wittman is one of the most decorated striking coaches in MMA history: 3x World MMA Awards Coach of the Year, 27 years of coaching experience, and a championship track record with Rose Namajunas (2x champion), Justin Gaethje (interim LW champion), and Kamaru Usman (multiple title defenses). His methodology emphasizes biomechanical efficiency, personalized game plans based on individual attributes, explosive power development, and mental preparation.

Wittman joined Sandhagen's team after the Nurmagomedov loss (Aug 2024) and was in camp for the Dvalishvili title fight. His pre-UFC 320 tease of an "unconventional grappling-based gameplan" to disrupt Dvalishvili's rhythm suggests Wittman had identified the defensive wrestling as insufficient and was attempting to reframe the approach. The result — 20 takedowns against — indicates that even with strategic preparation, the athletic/technical gap in wrestling against elite grapplers has not been closed.

For the Bautista camp (Nov 2025–July 2026 window), Wittman's challenge is specific: Bautista is a genuine grappling threat but not in Dvalishvili or Nurmagomedov's tier of wrestling dominance. Bautista averages 2.24 TD/15 min vs. Dvalishvili's approximately 4-5 TD/15 min. The coaching task is more manageable, but the underlying takedown defense issue remains. Wittman's striking development will be focused on maintaining range, using the front kick/teep as a cage prevention tool, and creating early-round finishing opportunities before Bautista can establish his rhythm.

*(Sources: ONX Sports Trevor Wittman Profile, MMAUnit Coach Profile, The Fight Site coaching breakdown, Blood Elbow pre-UFC 320)*

### Training Partners

Elevation Fight Team / High Altitude Martial Arts provides high-quality domestic training. Key training partners include:
- Neil Magny (welterweight veteran — provides elite grappling/wrestling rounds)

*(Sources: EssentiallySports gym profile, ONX Sports training content, Yahoo Sports Wittman interview)*

3. **Priority #3 (possible):** Offensive wrestling to disrupt Bautista's rhythm — the Font fight (TD every round) showed this is available; using takedowns offensively disrupts an opponent's offensive wrestling timing
4. **Wild card:** Clinch wrestling — Wittman may install specific cage escapes and underhook fighting given the back-take concern

## 10. Historical Context

### Grappling Losses Pattern (Sterling, Nurmagomedov, Dvalishvili)

The three-fight grappling pattern is the defining narrative of Sandhagen's career ceiling:

**Sterling (2020):** First exposure to elite UFC grappling. He was submitted by RNC in 1:28 — the fastest the vulnerability has ever been exposed. Sterling caught a kick, entered the clinch in a single movement, took the back within seconds, and locked in the choke. Sandhagen had no answer and was unconscious on the mat. This fight established that against elite BJJ athletes who can get to the back immediately, Sandhagen's submission defense is inadequate.

**Nurmagomedov (2024):** A more nuanced loss. Sandhagen actually showed improved defensive wrestling — he defended multiple takedowns and limited actual control time. But Nurmagomedov's wrestling threat dictated the terms of the entire fight: Sandhagen spent his energy defending rather than attacking, his striking output dropped because he was managing grappling angles, and Nurmagomedov's improved striking (52% accuracy vs. Sandhagen's 44%) exploited the defensive posture. The scorecards (50-45, 49-46, 49-46) reflect a comfortable decision. This is the most analytically concerning loss because even with preparation and improved defense, the outcome was the same.

**Dvalishvili (2025):** The ceiling-defining fight. 20 takedowns in 5 rounds — a UFC championship fight record. Wittman's "unconventional" game plan of seeking grappling exchanges failed. Sandhagen was competitive early, showed good scrambles, but could not solve the pace. The 49-45 x2, 49-46 cards suggest Sandhagen may have stolen a round or two but was comprehensively dominated. At the championship level against an elite wrestler with elite cardio, the takedown defense collapses over the course of 25 minutes.

**Pattern conclusion:** The loss to Sterling was a technique issue. The loss to Nurmagomedov was partially a game plan issue. The loss to Dvalishvili was a structural ceiling issue. The progression from "he can fix this" to "this is who he is" is complete.

*(Sources: Bloody Elbow UFC 250, Yahoo Sports Nurmagomedov analysis, CBS Sports UFC 320)*

### Fight 1 vs. Bautista (Jan 19, 2019 — UFC on ESPN+ 1, Brooklyn)

**Result:** Sandhagen won, Submission (Armbar), Round 1, 3:31

**Fight sequence:** Sandhagen was the aggressor from the opening bell, mixing kicks and punches effectively. He landed a flying knee that staggered Bautista and drove the fight to the ground. Working for a kimura from top position, Sandhagen transitioned, forced Bautista to the canvas, stepped over, and secured the armbar — Bautista tapped immediately. It was Bautista's promotional debut.

**Critical context for the rematch:** Sandhagen won with his wrestling, not his striking. The flying knee was not the finish — it was the setup that opened the ground sequence. The armbar came from offensive grappling, showing Sandhagen's submission capability. However, Bautista was a raw, untested debuting fighter absorbing a head shot before being taken down. He has since stated the armbar "hole" is the first thing he addressed in subsequent training.

## 11. Key Paths to Victory

**Path 1 — Early KO/TKO (Most Likely Win Scenario, ~35% of fight outcomes):**

Bautista must close distance to initiate grappling — every time he steps in, the flying knee is live. The Edgar KO (0:28) was executed against a forward-moving opponent, exactly how Bautista will approach the opening minutes. If Sandhagen can catch him with a leaping technique in the first exchange sequence, the fight ends.

**Path 2 — Decision Victory by Sustained Striking Output (~20% of fight outcomes):**
Sandhagen maintains distance for three rounds, lands 100+ significant strikes, and wins a clear decision. Bautista's takedown defense (historically better than many give credit for) needs to fail entirely, and Sandhagen needs to stay off the cage. This path requires Bautista to have a suboptimal night in his grappling entries, which is possible but requires specific conditions — primarily, that Bautista's timing on shot attempts is predictable and Sandhagen's front kick/teep shuts down entry attempts repeatedly.

**Path 3 — Offensive Wrestling Disruption (~5% of fight outcomes):**
Sandhagen attempts offensive takedowns of his own to disrupt Bautista's rhythm. The Font fight showed he can execute this game plan. If Bautista is focused on his own wrestling and Sandhagen beats him to the shot, Bautista's own takedown defense (45-47% accuracy on offense, moderate defense) may be exposed. This path is unlikely as a primary strategy but could appear as a mid-fight adjustment.

## 12. X-Factors

**X-Factor 1 — Flying Knee Timing Against Charging Opponent:**
Bautista will drive forward to initiate grappling. Every forward drive is a flying knee opportunity. If Sandhagen lands a clean flying knee in round 1, this fight is over regardless of the structural analysis. This is the primary reason Sandhagen ML at -200 is not an automatic fade — he can finish at any moment.

**X-Factor 2 — Bautista's Post-Nurmagomedov Psychology:**
Bautista was dominated by Nurmagomedov at UFC 321 in the same way Sandhagen was dominated (takedowns, back control, decision loss). He bounced back quickly with the Oliveira submission and is clearly motivated, but his confidence after experiencing Nurmagomedov-level grappling dominance may affect how aggressively he initiates grappling exchanges early — or it may have hardened him. Unknown variable.

**X-Factor 3 — The Armbar Scar Tissue:**
Bautista has stated he has fixed the armbar hole since 2019. But there is a psychological element — fighters who were submitted quickly in their career debut sometimes show hypervigilance about the specific submission they lost to, creating other openings. Alternatively, the specific trauma can produce hesitation at critical moments. This is speculative but historically relevant in rematches.

**X-Factor 4 — 3-Round vs. 5-Round Format:**
This is a 3-round fight, not a 5-round main event. Sandhagen's grappling defense losses were all 5-round fights where the sustained pressure over 25 minutes was the decisive factor. Bautista has 15 minutes to impose grappling control, not 25. Sandhagen's cardio advantage matters less, but more critically, the finish window for Bautista is compressed. Three rounds at a wrestling-heavy pace is significantly more manageable for Sandhagen's defensive grappling than five.

**X-Factor 5 — Trevor Wittman's Specific Adjustment:**
Wittman is among the top 3 coaches in MMA for fight-specific preparation. He had 8+ months to build a camp specifically around Bautista's grappling entries, cage sequences, and submission setups. Wittman-trained fighters have historically shown targeted improvements in individual fights (Namajunas vs. Andrade 2, Gaethje vs. Chandler). The adjustment could be specific and effective.
